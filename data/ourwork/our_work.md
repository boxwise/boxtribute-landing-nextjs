problem:
  label: The Problem
  title: Local organisations are usually first to respond - and last to receive direct funding.
  text: "<p>For decades, humanitarian aid has mostly worked like this: a big international
    organisation flies in, runs the response, and leaves when it's done. Local organisations
    - the ones actually based in the country, who know the community, speak the language,
    already have trust - often get sidelined, or are just used as small delivery partners.</p><p>\"Localisation\"

    the response themselves, with international actors playing a supporting role - funding,

structural_gap:
  label: The Structural Gap
  title: The Structural Gap
  text: Localisation doesn't fall short only because of capacity, or because funders
    and INGOs don't believe it's the way forward. One major, addressable cause is
    structural - the accountability infrastructure that makes direct funding possible
    has typically only been within reach of large, well-resourced organisations. It's
    part of a larger gap - not a failure on either side.
  items:
  - number: "01"
    title: Risk-Sharing
    text: Donors want to share financial and operational risk with local actors - but
      that depends on real-time visibility that they can rely on, not periodic reporting
      after the fact.
    color: navy
  - number: "02"
    title: Scale
    text: Donors need proof that a local team can grow fast enough to meet a crisis -
      without losing the oversight or accountability that comes easily to larger organisations.
      Speed and control aren't usually treated as compatible, but they can be.
    color: red
  - number: "03"
    title: Efficiency
    text: In a tighter funding climate, accountability isn't paperwork. Done well, it
      directly affects how much aid actually reaches the people its meant for.
    color: navy
